Does Your Accountant Check Your Books Before Taxes?

Here is a belief that quietly costs small business owners a lot of money: "I keep my own books, but my accountant will catch anything I got wrong when they do my taxes." It feels reasonable. It is also almost never true. Your tax preparer builds a return from whatever you hand them. They are not going through your books making sure everything is right, and they are not hunting for the deductions you missed. That is not their job. It is the bookkeeper's job. And if you are the bookkeeper, it is yours.

What a tax preparer actually does

A tax preparer takes the numbers you give them, your profit and loss, your balance sheet, your totals, and uses them to prepare and file a return. That is the engagement. In fact, most tax engagement letters say it in plain language: the return is based on information provided by the client, and the preparer is not auditing, reviewing, or verifying that information. They are trusting that your books are right.

So if your books say you spent $6,000 on "supplies" when half of that was actually equipment that should have been capitalized, the preparer does not know that. They see $6,000 of supplies, and that is what goes on the return. Garbage in, garbage out, except the garbage is now filed with the IRS under your name.

Your tax preparer prepares a return from your numbers. They do not check that your numbers are right. That gap is where money is lost.

Where this costs you

The damage runs in both directions, and neither one is good.

  • Missed deductions. If an expense is miscategorized, buried, or never recorded, it does not make it onto the return. You do not get the deduction, and you pay more tax than you owed. The preparer will not go looking for it, because they do not know it exists.
  • Things capitalized wrong. New businesses especially get this backwards. Equipment, improvements, and startup costs often have to be capitalized and depreciated over time, not expensed all at once. Get it wrong in either direction and your return is wrong, your depreciation is wrong, and it follows you for years.
  • Overstated or understated income. Booked a loan as income? Netted your merchant fees out of sales? Your taxable income is now fiction, and you either overpay or set yourself up for a problem later.
  • Audit exposure. A return built on messy books is a return with numbers that do not tie out. That is exactly the kind of thing that draws attention you do not want.

Why the two jobs are actually separate

This is not your tax preparer being lazy. Bookkeeping and tax preparation are two different disciplines with two different purposes. Bookkeeping is the year-round work of recording every transaction correctly, reconciling every account, and making sure the books reflect what really happened. Tax preparation is the once-a-year work of turning finished, correct books into a filed return. A good tax preparer can do wonders with clean books. No tax preparer can fix dirty books they were never hired to look at.

When those two jobs live in the same person and they have the time, great. But most small business owners hand a tax preparer a QuickBooks file, assume it has been vetted, and it has not. The vetting is a separate step that simply never happened.

The fix: get your books checked before they go to your preparer

The move is simple. Before you hand your books to whoever files your taxes, have someone who actually knows bookkeeping confirm they are right. Are your expenses categorized correctly? Was anything capitalized that should have been, or expensed that should not have been? Did every deduction make it in? Do your accounts reconcile to the actual statements? That review is the difference between a return built on facts and a return built on hope.

This matters most for new businesses, where the first year sets the pattern for everything after it, and where capitalization, startup costs, and owner money are easiest to get wrong. Clean up year one and every year after is easier. Get year one wrong and you carry it forward.

Common questions

Does my tax preparer check my books before filing my taxes?

Usually not in the way owners expect. Most preparers take the numbers you or your software hand them and put them on the return, and they are not paid to reconcile your bank accounts or catch miscoded transactions. That is the bookkeeper's job during the year, so by the time the return is prepared the books are already clean and the preparer can trust them.

What is the difference between a bookkeeper and a tax preparer?

A bookkeeper records and reconciles your transactions all year so your financials are accurate, while a tax preparer takes finished financials once a year and files the return. We are the bookkeeping side, so we do not file your taxes, but we keep everything tax-ready so your preparer has clean numbers to work from. When the two roles talk to each other, you get fewer surprises and a smoother filing.

Why do I still get tax surprises if I have an accountant?

Most tax surprises come from books that were never reconciled during the year, so the profit number on the return is different from what anyone expected. When your books are kept current and accurate month to month, your income and expenses are correct long before filing season. That lets you see your real profit early and set money aside instead of scrambling in April.
