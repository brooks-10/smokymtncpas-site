Is Your Shopify Payout the Same as Your Revenue?

Short answer: no. And treating it like it is one of the most common ways Shopify sellers end up with books that quietly lie to them. If you run a store and you book your Shopify payouts as your sales, your numbers are wrong before you even start, and usually wrong in a way that makes you look less profitable than you actually are.

What's actually inside a Shopify payout

When Shopify deposits money into your bank account, that deposit is not a clean pile of sales. It is a net number, what's left after Shopify already pulled several things out. A single payout can contain:

  • Gross sales (the actual revenue)
  • Minus payment processing fees
  • Minus refunds you issued
  • Minus chargebacks and disputes
  • Sometimes minus a reserve Shopify is holding
  • Plus or minus timing, sales near the end of a period often land in the next payout

So the number that hits your bank is real, but it is not your revenue. It is your revenue with a handful of other things baked in.

Why booking the payout as revenue breaks your books

If you record the payout as your sales, two things go wrong at once. First, your revenue is understated, because the fees and refunds got netted out before you ever saw the number. Second, and worse, those fees and refunds now have nowhere to live in your books, so you lose all visibility into what your store is actually spending on processing and losing to returns.

Your Shopify payout is not your revenue. We know the difference, down to the penny, and so should your books.

A real example

We looked at one store that had been booking payouts as revenue for a year. Buried inside those net deposits was roughly $40,000 a year in payment and platform fees that had never been recorded as an expense. The owner had no idea. Their revenue looked smaller than it was, their expenses looked smaller than they were, and their profit picture was fiction in both directions.

How to do it right

The fix is to break every payout back into its parts:

  • Book gross sales to revenue, the full amount customers actually paid, not the net deposit.
  • Book fees to their own expense account, so you can see exactly what processing is costing you.
  • Book refunds and chargebacks correctly, as contra-revenue, not as a mystery reduction.
  • Reconcile the net back to the actual bank deposit, to the penny, so nothing is missing.

Tools like A2X are built for exactly this, they take the raw Shopify data and turn it into entries that separate sales from fees from refunds. But the tool is only as good as the setup behind it, which is where a lot of DIY books quietly go wrong.

Why it's worth getting right

This is not accounting for accounting's sake. When your books separate sales from fees, you finally know your real margin, you can see whether processing costs are creeping up, your sales tax ties out, and the profit number you're using to decide what you can pay yourself is actually true. Get the payout wrong and every decision downstream is built on a number that isn't real.

Common questions

Is my Shopify payout the same as my revenue?

No. A Shopify payout is what lands in your bank after Shopify takes out processing fees, refunds, and sometimes chargebacks, so it is always smaller than what your customers actually paid. Your true revenue is the full amount of the sales, and the fees and refunds are separate expenses that belong on their own lines. If you book the payout as revenue, you understate both your sales and your costs.

Why is the money in my bank less than my Shopify sales?

Shopify nets out payment processing fees, refunds, and any holds before it sends the payout to your bank, so the deposit is your sales minus those deductions. Recording only the deposit hides how much you really sold and how much you paid in fees. Proper Shopify bookkeeping splits each payout back into its parts so your revenue, fees, and refunds each show up correctly.

How should Shopify payouts be recorded in bookkeeping?

Each payout should be broken down into gross sales, sales tax collected, processing fees, refunds, and any adjustments, rather than posted as one lump deposit. Tools like A2X do this automatically by matching the payout to the underlying orders, which keeps your revenue and margins accurate. Getting this right is the single most common fix we make on Shopify books.
