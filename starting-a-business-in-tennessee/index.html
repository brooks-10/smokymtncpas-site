Starting a Business in Tennessee: What to Do First

Smoky Mountain CPAs

Tennessee is one of the more welcoming states to start a business. No personal income tax, moderate fees, and a fast online filing process. Welcoming does not mean simple, though. A few early choices shape your taxes, your paperwork, and your personal risk for years. Here is the order we walk new owners through.

Pick your structure

Your legal structure is the first decision to get right. A sole proprietorship is the simplest, with no state filing, but you and the business are legally the same, so your personal assets are exposed. A general partnership carries that same risk, split between owners.

Most small businesses in Tennessee choose an LLC. It separates your personal assets from business debts, and profits pass through to your own tax return without a separate corporate tax. A corporation is more formal, with a board and set rules to follow, and it usually makes sense only if you plan to raise outside money or bring on many owners.

Talk to your CPA before you file. Starting right is much easier than fixing it later.

Register with the state

Once you have picked a structure, make it official. Check that your name is available on the Secretary of State's database first. Then file your formation document, Articles of Organization for an LLC, through the state's online portal. Every LLC and corporation also needs a registered agent with a physical Tennessee address to receive legal and state mail.

Sole proprietors and partnerships do not file formation documents, but if you operate under a name other than your own, you file an assumed name with the county register of deeds. Keep your filing confirmation. You will need that number for your EIN, your bank account, and your licenses.

Get an EIN and open a business bank account

Apply for an EIN from the IRS. It is free and takes minutes online, and it keeps your Social Security number off your business paperwork. Then open a dedicated business bank account and card as soon as you have your EIN and formation papers.

Run every business expense through those accounts. Mixing personal and business money is one of the fastest ways to lose your LLC protection and to turn your books into a mess. Consider a separate savings account for setting aside taxes as you earn.

Know your Tennessee taxes

No personal income tax surprises new owners into thinking there is little state tax to plan for. There is plenty.

Franchise and excise tax applies to most LLCs and corporations doing business here. As of 2026, franchise tax is based on net worth. Sales and use tax applies if you sell taxable goods or certain services, with a state rate of 7 percent plus local rates as of 2026. A separate local business tax applies to gross receipts, generally once you pass 100,000 dollars. Federal tax still sits on top of all of it. Rates and thresholds change and the details get specific fast, so confirm current figures with the Tennessee Department of Revenue and loop in your CPA before you file.

Handle licenses and local rules

Tennessee has no single business license. Most businesses need a county or city license tied to the local business tax. Some professions need a state board license on top of that, and some industries need special permits, such as food service or alcohol sales.

Before you sign a lease, confirm the location is zoned for your business and that you can get a certificate of occupancy. These rules are set locally, so they vary from one city or county to the next. Always check with the specific place where you will operate.

Set up your books and your insurance

Pick accounting software early and connect it to your business accounts from day one. Build a clean chart of accounts that fits your industry, and reconcile every account each month. Good books from the start can save you money and stress at tax time.

An LLC protects your personal assets, but it does not stop the business from being sued or damaged. That is what insurance is for. General liability, property, and, once you have employees, workers' compensation are the common starting points.

Plan for ongoing compliance

Starting the business is a one-time project. Staying in good standing is a yearly one. LLCs and corporations file an annual report with the Secretary of State, due April 1 for calendar-year filers. Add your franchise and excise return, your business license renewal, and any payroll filings. The best defense against a missed deadline is one calendar, shared with your CPA, that tracks every date in one place.

The bottom line

Tennessee is genuinely a friendly place to start a business. Friendly does not mean hands-off. A good CPA and a simple compliance calendar go a long way toward keeping you out of trouble.
